Don't Ban Conscious AI. It Is Our Duty to Try and Build It.

Microsoft's AI chief says to stop AI development if consciousness emerges in training. But there is no agreement on what the signs actually are; the responsible path is the one genetics took: try to build it, test it in the open under strict rules, and perhaps learn what consciousness is actually made of.

Imagine that in the 1950s, shortly after the structure of DNA was discovered, voices such as “We shouldn’t be tampering with creation”, “This will lead to cloning of humans”, or “we will soon have mutants” flooded the news, and halted all DNA research. There would be no lab-made human insulin; many genetic diseases could not be diagnosed, there would be no modern forensics, no fast vaccines, and no ancestral tracing of humans to their collective source.

In reality, scientists did pause some genetic-engineering experiments in 1974 over safety concerns. However, within two years rules were written, and the research went on. Another scare took place in 1997, when the cloning of Dolly the sheep was announced. Many governments then banned human cloning and allowed the rest of the research to continue. The research people feared became the source of real cures, knowledge and human advancement.

When safety was an issue, scientists paused, wrote the rules, and then did the work in the open. One might claim that the same must be done with concerns over AI consciousness. On November 1, Mustafa Suleyman, the head of Microsoft AI, warned against building conscious AI. He said today’s AIs “do not feel, experience, or suffer,” but added: “If consciousness started to emerge in training, I would call for everyone to stop AI development.”

In 1974, the famous DNA pause was due to concrete, tangible concerns: potential cancer gene spread, untreatable infections, and new toxic strains of bacteria. But what would a sign of consciousness look like in an AI model? A model that claims that it feels? Models say that all the time. They were trained on billions of sentences written by humans, and designed to sound like people. And the wrapper around a model’s engine shapes what it says about its own consciousness; I show this in my paper “Same Model, Different Wrapper” (preprint, Zenodo, Oct 11, 2026). In Skeptic last month, I called this the AI epistemic trap: the evidence comes from the very mechanism we are trying to evaluate.

The model’s words, then, can’t be the test. What else can we use? The model’s behavior won’t do, either. A model that “decides” to run away from a sandbox, resists being shut down or schemes in a test, is always doing what the test, the prompt, or the training set it up to do.

The idea, then, of halting development if signs appear, is a rule with no trigger. It will either never fire, or it will fire immediately at the first sign of what “feels” human.

The side that says machine consciousness is possible has a similar hole. The same week, The New York Times ran an opinion piece titled "Could A.I. Be Conscious? It's Definitely Possible." Its author, Toni Sims of NYU's Center for Mind, Ethics, and Policy, argues that we should take the possibility seriously and weigh the evidence, starting with what a system says and does. But that’s the same trap again. She does list other evidence, such as how a system works inside, which points in the right direction. Yet she concedes that each kind is limited, and hopes they add up together. Weak evidence piled up is not a test.

So, one side says stop if signs appear; the other says weigh the signs. Neither offers a test that could settle it.

Personally, I believe today’s machines are far from being conscious. We’ve done an exceptional job making them sound human, and we are now tripping over how good they sound. I also don’t believe scaling AI models up would change that. But belief, mine or anyone's, settles nothing. So, I propose a bar. In my upcoming book I argue that machine consciousness is a claim that must be earned, and that no one should suggest a machine might be conscious until there is evidence of two basic things human consciousness requires: a perception of space and a perception of time.

A perception of space is not sensors that report what is around a robot. It is a unified field of the present moment; an involuntary, always-on felt presence. You don’t decide to feel the chair under you or the cold draft from the window; it is simply there, all at once. A humanoid’s sensors might register the draft, but nothing in it feels the cold; at most, it responds by mimicking human behavior.

A perception of time is the basis of self-consciousness. It is awareness held from one moment to the next, so that experience has a before and an after, and time drags when you are bored and flies when you are having fun. An AI model might know what time it is, but time is merely a word it predicts.

How do I know a model doesn’t perceive time? I don’t. I also don’t know whether a hammer does. The only difference is that the model sounds human, and that is the trap: the very thing that makes the output persuasive is the thing that makes it worthless as evidence.

My proposal to Suleyman, Sims, and to everybody who says that machine consciousness is possible – build it. Don’t insinuate it appeared by accident, in a product designed to act conscious. But on purpose, in a lab, in the open. Give a machine an artificial present moment and an artificial sense of time. Log every computation, so researchers read the machinery instead of asking the machine how it feels. Then test it, not by what it says, but by what its architecture does. In my book, I outline how.

Refusing to build it does not make the concerns go away. It merely leaves the work to the worst possible hands: unmonitored commercial systems, criminal enterprises or rogue adversaries. We should test every real possibility of machine consciousness, under strict welfare and safety rules from the first day. I expect such tests to fail; I expect a machine with an artificial perception of space and time to still be a machine, with nothing it is like to be it. That failure would be the most useful result in the field: it would tell us that consciousness requires something we haven't named yet.

In the unlikely case it actually works, we will finally know what consciousness is made of. Like the discovery of DNA, that knowledge would not stay in the lab; it could change how we treat coma, anesthesia and dementia, to name a few, and would help us understand our own minds. The only option that teaches us nothing is the one Suleyman calls for: forbid the experiment, and keep reading the tea leaves of chat logs.

What makes us human, in part, is our relentless pursuit of knowledge and understanding of the self. The question of AI consciousness is forcing us to reevaluate the hard problem of human consciousness. That's a good thing. Until someone builds a machine that can perceive space and time, "conscious" is a claim we should earn, not throw around.

Erez Aeon Batat is a consciousness researcher and AI consultant, founder of Aeongenics and a former Director of Innovation at HP. He is the author of the forthcoming book Solve for Human: What Makes Us Conscious, and Why AI Isn't (Yet).
