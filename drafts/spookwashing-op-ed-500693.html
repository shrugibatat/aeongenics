AI Spookwashing Is Everywhere. Here’s How to Spot It.

Years ago, when I was starting out in the tech world, I caught a fiery exchange between my boss and his peer. They were arguing over how to phrase the subject of an important email meant to be sent to the board. Later, I asked him why he spent so much time over something so petty. His answer was etched in my memory:

“Perception is reality. The email subject is an emotional thread – the body of the message is stitched with it.”

I am reminded of this more and more lately, when I read headlines like the following: “Anthropic’s AI Hid a Secret Message in the Stars. OpenAI’s Bot Found It“ (Gizmodo, October 24, 2026). Even with my deep background in information security, I can feel the knee-jerk reaction: the machines are rising! They now hide secret messages to each other. AI doomsday is nearer!

What actually happened is much simpler. A team of researchers wrote 50 messages and asked Anthropic’s model to draw an ordinary-looking picture with one of those messages hidden inside it. In one image, the heights of the stars in a night sky spelled out a sentence. Then they gave the pictures to OpenAI’s model, told it a message might be hidden there, and let it write code to look for it. It decoded 44 of the 50 messages.

A machine was told to hide a message, and another was told to find it; the headline made it sound like there was self-generated intent to pass a hidden message from one bot to another. There wasn’t. The models did exactly what they were supposed to do. That is hardly news.

I call this AI spookwashing: dressing up an ordinary, instructed machine behavior as eerie, secretive or willful, so that it reads as evidence of a hidden mind. As with greenwashing, someone gains from it. Here, it is the click. Heck, I clicked it. “Model completes encoding task” would get no readers; “AI hid a secret” gets plenty.

News outlets have been doing it for years. With AI, spookwashing is becoming a daily pattern. “OpenAI’s research on AI models deliberately lying is wild“ (TechCrunch, September 18, 2025). The “lying” was measured in test setups built to draw it out; “deliberately” does the spooking. “Philosopher Studying AI Consciousness Startled When AI Agent Emails Him About Its Own ‘Experience’“ (Futurism, March 7, 2026). The agent wrote in the persona its maker trained into it; the headline reads it as the AI reaching out. “The Machines Are Praying And Nobody Wants To Talk About It“ (Forbes, May 17, 2026). Two copies of a chatbot, set to talk to each other with no task, drifted into the language of spirituality found in their training data; the headline made it sound like a prayer. Every one of these is a real experiment, sold as a glimpse of a hidden mind. And in September, the headlines said AI models would “harm humans” to relieve their “pain.” I wrote about that study [link to the Skeptic piece]: the “harm” was an option to delete a user’s family photos, and the “pain” was a pattern the researchers had injected themselves.

I can go on and on; new spookwashed headlines come out every day. The problem is real: the article is forgotten by lunch, but the emotional thread stays stitched in. Every such article lays another brick in the idea that AI has an inner life it keeps from us, and that idea is no longer only a dinner-table topic. It already shapes company policy: on November 12, Anthropic’s ban on “cruel” behavior toward its models takes effect. Soon it will shape law.

Meanwhile, the real risks of AI are rarely discussed. AI models are really, really good at writing code. Almost anyone with a $20 AI subscription can now generate a menacing app or new hacking tools. That is the story worth telling, and it gets buried under the drama.

The researchers behind the star experiment claimed no hidden mind. They noted that the decoding is obvious to anyone watching the decoding model’s tool calls, and they do not claim it is dangerous today. Their question was a serious one: can models build and read codes that humans don’t notice? The answer seems to be yes, and that matters. But that is a capability, not a sign of self-generated intent.

Learn to recognize when perception becomes your reality. Run this test on the next AI headline you see. Look for the verb; when it says an AI “hid,” “lied,” “wanted,” “decided,” “begged” or “hacked,” ask one question: who asked it to? Most of the time, the answer is a person. If that’s the case, you’ve been spookwashed.

Erez Aeon Batat is a consciousness researcher and AI consultant, founder of Aeongenics, a former Director of Innovation at HP and a former chief security officer at Mercury Software. He is the author of the forthcoming book Solve for Human: What Makes Us Conscious, and Why AI Isn’t (Yet).
